Air Quality: From Global Patterns to Melbourne

Air quality: a measure of how clean or polluted the air is, and how safe it is to breathe. This data visualisation starts with a world view of how cities compare with the World Health Organisation (WHO) guideline, then looks at the toll on human health, and finally zooms in on air quality across Melbourne and Victoria, to investigate air quality trends in the data.

PM2.5
Particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres across.
µg/m³
Micrograms of particles in one cubic metre of air.
WHO guideline
An annual average of 5 µg/m³ for PM2.5 (2021 update).

A Global Overview

We begin with the biggest picture: how clean is the air in cities around the world, and what does polluted air cost the people who breathe it?

Very few cities meet the WHO guideline

This map shows how far each city’s most recent annual PM2.5 level (2024 or 2025) sits above the WHO 2021 guideline. Teal dots meet the guideline, and the colours darken through yellow, orange and red as pollution reaches seven or more times the limit. The darkest clusters appear across East and South Asia, while many European cities sit in the lighter orange range. Very few cities worldwide are teal, which shows that safe air is the exception.

Each dot is one city. Use the dropdown to change the pollutant.

At the extremes: the best and worst cities for PM10

The same gap shows up when cities are ranked directly. This chart uses PM10, a coarser particle (up to 10 micrometres), and the dashed line marks the WHO guideline of 15 µg/m³. The five worst cities all record annual means of about 98 to 100 µg/m³, more than six times the guideline. Four of the five cleanest cities record less than 2 µg/m³, and three of them are in Norway. Türkiye appears at both ends of the ranking.

Change is uneven: Türkiye worsened while Norway improved

Zooming in on a handful of countries, this connected dot plot compares median city PM2.5 in 2024 (grey) with 2025 (orange). The dashed line marks the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. Change is not equal around the world. For example, Türkiye rose sharply to about 28 µg/m³ (worsening air quality), while Norway dropped to around 1 µg/m³ (improving air quality). Only Norway and Estonia end 2025 at or below the guideline.

Use the dropdown to switch to other pollutants.

Polluted air has a human cost

After looking at global air quality trends, it is important to remember that air pollution is not just a statistic; bad air quality takes a serious toll on population health. This map shows where that toll is greatest, measured as deaths attributed to air pollution per 100,000 people in 2021. The darkest shades, with more than 100 deaths per 100,000 people, cluster across Eastern Europe, parts of Africa, and South and East Asia. Australia, Canada and the Nordic countries sit at the lightest end, with fewer than 20 deaths per 100,000 people. Compared with the first map, places with high pollution often also suffer high death rates.

Grey areas have no data.

Total deaths rise with population, but the risk per person varies

Each bubble is a country, plotted by population (horizontal) and total deaths (vertical) on logarithmic scales, so each step along an axis is ten times larger than the last. Bubble size shows the death rate per 100,000 people, and colour shows the WHO region. India and China sit in the top right because of their large populations. Smaller countries such as Singapore, New Zealand and Australia sit lower, with smaller bubbles. Reading size as well as position separates countries with many deaths because they are large from countries where each person faces a higher risk.

Use the population slider and region dropdown to filter the view and compare countries of similar size.

Zooming In: Melbourne and Victoria

The global picture shows that safe air is rare. Next we look closely at one place: the EPA Victoria air-monitoring sites across Melbourne and regional Victoria during 2025.

Melbourne’s air sits close to the guideline all year

Moving from the global scale to Melbourne, the map shows average air quality at each monitoring site, with larger bubbles meaning higher PM2.5. The lollipop chart then shows how the monthly mean across all sites changed through 2025. Levels peaked at around 7.7 µg/m³ in May and dipped to roughly 4.3 µg/m³ in September and November. Even the lowest months sit close to the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³.

Most days are low, but sharp spikes stand out

This line chart tracks the daily mean PM2.5 across the year, with an overview strip below it. Most days stay below 10 µg/m³, but sharp spikes stand out, including a peak of about 26 µg/m³ in mid-May. Brushing lets you investigate these spikes and see whether they are one-off events or part of a pattern.

Drag across the overview strip to zoom into any period. Use the site dropdown to view a single monitoring site.

Where and when: city traffic and winter heating

Each row is a monitoring site and each column is a month, so you can compare locations and seasons at a glance. Melbourne CBD and Traralgon are consistently among the darkest, with Traralgon highest in the winter months. City centres like Melbourne’s often have high particulate matter because of traffic and dense urban activity, which contributes to air pollution all year round. Traralgon, a regional town, does not have the same urban activity, but its winter increase may be linked to factors such as more wood being burned for heating in the colder months.

White cells mark missing data, such as Footscray before October.

Bushfire smoke reached Melbourne after the fires began

Natural disasters like wildfires can increase air pollution and fine particulate matter, as smoke, ash and organic carbon particles drift across regions. In early 2025, dry lightning started fires in two new sections of the Grampians National Park and at Little Desert National Park in regional Victoria. Particles from these fires affected air quality in metropolitan areas around Melbourne. The fires began on 27 January 2025 and were contained by early February, but PM2.5 in metropolitan Victoria rose slightly later, with the darkest days falling between 3 and 8 February.

What the data shows

Across the world, clean air is rare and the human cost is high, with the heaviest burden falling on parts of Eastern Europe, Africa and Asia. Melbourne’s annual levels sit much closer to the WHO guideline, but they are not fixed: winter conditions, urban activity and events such as the early 2025 bushfires can push daily levels well above it.
